# Patient education and shared-decision framework

## Source anchors

- [WHO health literacy](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/health-literacy) emphasizes accessible, timely, appropriate information and organizational support.
- [NICE NG197 shared decision making](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng197/chapter/recommendations) supports choice, option and decision talk; understandable language; alternatives; chunk-and-check; and teach-back.
- [World Physiotherapy informed consent](https://world.physio/policy/ps-consent) expects appropriate consent before physiotherapy is undertaken.

## Plain-language transformation

Preserve the clinical meaning while changing presentation:

- start with the person's functional concern;
- use common words before technical labels;
- define necessary terms once;
- use active voice and concrete actions;
- use short chunks and informative headings;
- pair verbal explanation with demonstration or visual structure when useful;
- avoid unexplained abbreviations, “compliance” blame, deterministic anatomy, and vague commands such as “strengthen your core.”

Reading-level tools are prompts for revision, not proof that information is understandable.

## Shared-decision sequence

1. **Choice talk:** make clear that reasonable options exist and participation is welcome.
2. **Option talk:** compare purpose, benefits, harms, burden, uncertainty, and no-treatment/no-change where relevant.
3. **Decision talk:** explore what matters, preferences, practical constraints, and readiness; agree on a next step.

Do not steer with fear, hide uncertainty, or present a decision aid as a replacement for clinician discussion.

## Risk communication

Prefer:

- absolute risk with denominator and time period;
- consistent denominator across options;
- natural frequencies when easier to understand;
- both possible benefit and harm;
- uncertainty range and evidence certainty;
- baseline risk and applicability caveat.

Avoid relative risk alone, mismatched denominators, vague “low/high risk,” and unsupported personalized probability.

## Teach-back

Use a non-testing frame:

> “I want to check that I explained it clearly. In your own words, what is the plan, and what would make you stop and seek help?”

For an action, ask for demonstration when safe. Correct the explanation, simplify, demonstrate again, and recheck. Document what was understood and any remaining barrier.

## Rehabilitation instruction template

- purpose linked to the person's goal;
- setup, equipment, space, assistance;
- step-by-step action;
- approved dose and rest;
- expected effort/sensation;
- common errors and a simple cue;
- progression only when pre-agreed;
- hold/stop/escalation signs;
- tracking and follow-up.

## Behavior support

Use collaborative strategies: capability/opportunity/motivation barriers, implementation intentions, environment design, graded goals, feedback, social support, and meaningful rewards. Avoid moralizing adherence. If the plan is unrealistic, change the plan.

## Population and accessibility adaptations

- **Child:** age-appropriate language/play, guardian information, child assent and voice.
- **Aphasia/cognitive need:** one step at a time, pictures/demonstration, supported conversation, caregiver only with consent.
- **Vision/hearing:** high contrast/large type, audio/captions, quiet environment, interpreter or assistive support.
- **Pain/distress:** validate experience, avoid threat amplification, explain uncertainty without dismissal.
- **Remote:** confirm privacy, comprehension, safe environment, helper, and emergency plan.

Do not use family as an interpreter when a qualified interpreter is required by the setting or law.

## Visual explanation

Use a flow for action sequences, a two-column table for options, a timeline for recovery phases, or a simple body/function/activity map. Do not use visuals to imply diagnostic certainty or structural damage unsupported by evidence.
